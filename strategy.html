Portfolio revamp: the reasoning behind every call.

I've read your portfolio the way a design director hiring for a senior role would. Here is what I found, what I changed and why, and what only you can fix.

01Verdict

Your thinking is already top-tier. Your packaging wasn't.

The source material is better than most senior portfolios I'd expect to see. You have real decisions, rejected alternatives, honest evidence and working interactive recreations. Most designers can't write “what I shipped first, what broke, what shipped” for even one decision, and you have more than twenty.

What was holding it back wasn't the content. It was that the strongest material was hard to find in the first minute, and a reviewer usually decides in that minute whether to keep reading. Six problems:

  1. Your positioning was too generic for your work. “Enterprise systems people can operate without a manual” could describe hundreds of B2B designers. Your best decisions share a sharper theme that you never named. (Section 03.)
  2. The home page showed nothing for over two seconds. The hero fades in with a delay. On first load, a reviewer sees a beige page.
  3. The headline numbers measured volume, not impact. The home-page stats were “10 products, 73 policies, 62 users, 1,000+ employees.” A director reads those as activity. Your outcome numbers were buried inside case studies.
  4. The case studies were too long. HRM Genie has seven decisions and five interactive demos. Nobody reads that on a first pass, and the best decision (the rule-engine trade-off) sits fourth.
  5. The honesty became a nervous habit. “I'd rather show four honest numbers than ten I can't defend” appears in some form on almost every page. Said once, it's integrity. Said four times, it sounds anxious. I replaced it with a system (the evidence scale), so the honesty is structural and you only explain it once.
  6. The visual language was a well-made template. Inter, an italic serif accent, warm off-white and rounded cards is the 2024–25 default. Competent, but nobody remembers it.

02bErrors a reviewer would catch

Inconsistencies in the current site

Directors read closely. Each of these makes them doubt everything else on the page:

  • The home page says “The three above are where the thinking is” under five case studies.
  • MDM Genie's heading says “Two decisions that defined the product”, then shows four. TAT Genie says “Six decisions”, numbers two of them “Decision 04”, then says “Four decisions carry the narrative.”
  • The Sendcrux case study is labelled “03” but is fifth in the list.
  • “10 products taken 0→1” on the home page vs “15 products” on Journey.
  • MDM research is described as 5 admins “across three companies” in one place and “five organisations” in another.
  • 42% → 79% with n=5 doesn't work as a headcount: 42% of 5 people isn't a whole number. If it's an average on a willingness scale, say so. If it's 2 of 5 → 4 of 5, say “2 of 5 → 4 of 5.” This is the first thing a sharp interviewer will ask about, so resolve it before you send the link. 03Positioning

The theme you never named

Put your best decisions side by side:

  • AI routes tickets only above a confidence threshold, and overrides become training data. (TAT)
  • The AI candidate score is broken into editable parts, because nobody acts on a number they can't question. (HRM)
  • Batch approval can't sweep up uncertain AI output. (QA)
  • Auto-assignment shows its reasoning, or people route around it. (TAT)
  • An extra step proves to employees what IT can't see. (MDM)
  • A blast-radius preview replaces “Are you sure?” (MDM)
  • Automate the labour, never the understanding. (Sendcrux)

Every one is about the line between what the system decides and what a person must be able to check. That's the central design problem of 2026: agents, copilots, automated workflows. Every AI lab and serious B2B company is hiring for it, and you have shipped evidence across five products.

Before

“I design enterprise systems people can operate without a manual.”

After

“I design software that makes decisions, and the parts that let people trust them.”

Why not lead with “Designer + PM” or “designer who codes”? Both are true and both are valuable, but they describe how you work, not what you're best at. At top companies, a dual title raises a levelling question (“is this a PM?”) that you don't want in the first screen. Both now appear as evidence: the rule-engine trade-off shows product judgement, and the working demos show engineering fluency.

Your elevator pitch, for intros, LinkedIn and the first minute of every interview:

“I design the trust layer of complex software: the moment an AI or an automated system asks a person to believe it. I've shipped that across AI triage, device security and HR policy engines, usually as the only designer, so I also own the specs and the front-end.”

04Research

What the strongest portfolios do, and what hiring managers say

From hiring managers (recent LinkedIn posts and portfolio-review guides; practitioner opinion, not formal research):

  • Put 2–4 of your strongest case studies up front, with clear headings and skimmable structure. Use the same structure in every case study so reviewers know where to look.
  • Start each case study with a summary and outcomes. Senior candidates should lead with impact, not a long tour of their process.
  • Polished screens without decisions or trade-offs are a reason to reject. Reviewers want some mess, as long as the line from problem to decision is clear.
  • If you can't share exact numbers, give direction and magnitude. At staff level, reviewers expect at least one example of moving a real business metric.
  • Show what a design system enabled, not the system itself. And show how you're experimenting with AI.

Patterns from the portfolios people remember, mostly design engineers and senior designers at product-led companies:

  • The portfolio demonstrates the skill instead of describing it. Craft-focused designers publish small, interactive pieces you can touch. Your coded recreations already do this. They just weren't above the fold.
  • Writing as proof of thinking. Senior designers at top companies are often known for a few essays more than a gallery. Your “principle” lines are essays waiting to be written.
  • Restraint. The strongest sites have the fewest projects. Curation signals judgement.
  • One idea, executed fully. Memorable sites have a single concept (a format, an interaction, a voice) carried through everything, rather than many effects.

Sources for the hiring-manager points: read.cv/twanlass, Exponent portfolio guide, Drexel UXID “Don't Make Me Read”, Bootcamp on Medium, HackerNoon, and hiring-manager posts on LinkedIn. The portfolio patterns come from my own knowledge of the field, not a fresh crawl, so treat them as direction, not citation.

InterviewDepth

“Also worth asking me about” lists what stays out of the page.

The ten-chapter story. Every case study follows the same chapters, tracked by a sticky chapter bar: project overview (audience and business context), problem (with a “how might we”), research and discovery (methods, numbered findings, limitations), product strategy (bets and explicit trade-offs), information architecture (module map, journeys, system layers), UX and UI design (decision records, rejected-vs-shipped wireframe explorations, working demos), key features, challenges and solutions, impact (graded outcomes and who benefits), and my role (a contribution matrix that separates what I owned from what I shared, plus reflection). NDA screens are replaced by diagrams and wireframes that show the thinking, and every claim traces to the original portfolio or a public product page.

The Decision Record borrows from engineering's Architecture Decision Records, which signals systems thinking to technical leaders. Each one has: context → options (rejected ones marked ✕, the shipped one marked ●) → evidence grade → one-sentence principle. Because the structure is the same everywhere, a reviewer learns it once.

The evidence scale (●●● measured, ●●○ usability-tested, ●○○ stakeholder-reported, ○○○ scope) replaces paragraphs of caveats. It makes your integrity visible at a glance and is something reviewers will remember you for.

The Decision Index on the home page lists 16 decisions, filterable by AI, trust and safety, systems, and strategy. A hiring manager for an AI-agent team can go straight to your five AI decisions. As far as I know, no standard portfolio template does this.

Ranking. MDM goes first because it has the strongest research reversal and your best number. TAT is second because it's the most relevant to AI hiring and shows you influencing a roadmap you didn't own. HRM is third because it has your clearest product trade-off. QA (04) and Sendcrux (05) use the same card format as the first three, so the showcase reads as one series. They are shorter case studies: QA has no outcome data yet, and Sendcrux is older but has live, public proof. Five equal cards is a trade-off against curation, so keep the order: strongest first.

06Creative direction

Concept: “show the decision”

The memorable thing isn't an animation. It's that the site behaves like your thesis. From any page, a visitor can interview you through the floating chat button in the bottom-right corner. “Interview me in 60 seconds” is a chat where they tap a question (how you design for AI, your biggest mistake, a decision you defended, whether your numbers hold up). Each answer is written by you and comes with a proof card linking to the decision, the graded number or the quote behind it. It is clearly labelled “no AI”, so the format itself shows the trust thesis: nothing is invented. It answers screening-call questions before the call happens. Every case-study demo works the same way, with a v1 and a v2 you can switch between.

  • Design language: aligned to GrowthSchool (at your request). The system follows growthschool.io's tokens and components. It uses none of their logo, imagery or copy.
  • Type. IBM Plex Sans throughout: 500 weight for headings (48 / 32 / 20px scale), 400 for body, uppercase 14px eyebrows. The second half of each headline drops to 400 weight in softer grey instead of switching typeface. IBM Plex Mono only for timestamps and DNS records inside demos.
  • Colour. Deep teal #002726 ink, cream #FFFAF1 cards and section bands, one green action colour #33C375 (a darker #147A43 wherever green is text, for contrast), peach #FFE3B1 for quote marks, and a #042626 footer. Light mode only, as on GrowthSchool.
  • Components. A dark teal hero card with a 48px grid-line texture. A cream dotted card for the live demo. White uppercase chips with teal borders. A green primary button with an inset teal ring. Cream cards with 12px or 24px radii. FAQ-style bordered rows for the Decision Index. A sticky cream action bar at the bottom of every page.
  • Motion. Only motion that carries meaning: rows reorder with FLIP so you see the queue change, countdowns are live, and undo windows really count down. Content is never hidden waiting for an animation, and reduced-motion settings are respected.
  • Layout. A 1152px container. Case studies keep a sticky numbered section label on the left, so long cases stay navigable.

What I deliberately didn't do: WebGL heroes, custom cursors, scroll-jacking or a bento grid. They make portfolios memorable for the wrong reason, and on a page that argues for trust and clarity, they'd contradict the argument.

08Only you can do these

Before you send the link

  1. Resolve the 42% → 79% math.State what was measured: a scale average, or 2/5 → 4/5. Then update mdm.html and the home-page proof strip.
  2. Fix the research counts.5 admins across three companies or five organisations? Pick the true one.
  3. Add team composition to each case study.e.g. “1 PM, 4 engineers, 1 QA.” Hiring managers look for it to judge scope and collaboration. I left it out rather than guess.
  4. Get one instrumented metric from Brilyant.Even one real number, like TAT's SLA attainment or MDM's real BYOD enrolment rate, would turn a flagship case into staff-level evidence. Ask your PM for production data you're allowed to cite.
  5. Check NDA and naming permissions.You name the products and the employer. Confirm you're allowed to, and that the recreations don't expose anything the customers consider confidential.
  6. Align LinkedIn and your résumé.Headline: “Product Designer: AI, trust & complex systems.” Make the résumé's first line match the site's thesis. Inconsistency between them is a common reason reviewers lose confidence.
  7. Put it on your own domain.Real multi-page URLs give you deep links (e.g. /tat.html#d2) that you can drop into recruiter messages for specific roles.
  8. Record a 90-second walkthrough of one case study.Hiring managers increasingly use these to judge how you communicate. Link it from the MDM page.
  9. Write one essay.“Designing the override: what four AI products taught me about human-in-the-loop UX.” It's your thesis in long form, it's very shareable, and it gives interviewers something to talk to you about.
