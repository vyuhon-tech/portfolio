The 30-second versionFull story · 12 min read

Problem

The business needed employees to enrol their own phones. Only 42% said they would. The flow was fast, so speed wasn’t the issue. People didn’t believe what it said.

What I did

Reframed the brief from admin complexity to trust. Added a step that shows what IT can and can never see, cut several hundred possible controls to 73, and made dangerous actions show their blast radius first.

What changed

Stated willingness to enrol rose from 42% to 79% in testing. Three OS-specific consoles became one policy model.

01Project overview

A single console for three operating systems, and two kinds of user who want opposite things.

MDM Genie lets an IT team enrol devices, set security policy, monitor compliance and act remotely across Android, Windows and Apple fleets, from one place.

It serves two people whose interests genuinely conflict. The admin wants control and visibility. The employee wants privacy and autonomy. Most MDM tools resolve that tension by serving the buyer and ignoring the user, which is exactly why adoption stalls.

Primary user

IT admins

Configure policy, enrol devices and respond to incidents across mixed fleets, usually in three separate tools.

Secondary user

Employees on BYOD

Asked to enrol a personal phone for work, and worried about what IT will be able to see.

Stakeholder

Security & compliance

Customers are audited against compliance frameworks, so policy has to map to real requirements.

Business context

The metric the business cared about was employees enrolling their own phones, and it wasn’t moving. Customers also ran separate consoles per OS, so policy drifted between platforms and nobody had one view of compliance. Competitors answered with volume: 200+ configurable policies, of which admins used about a dozen.

02Problem

Enrolment was stuck, and nothing on screen showed admins the consequences of a click.

“I’m happy to enrol my phone for work. I just don’t want IT seeing my personal photos or my WhatsApp.”

BYOD participant · enrolment testing

3 toolsone per operating systemThe same security intent had to be configured three times and kept in sync by hand.
Incidentshow violations were foundAdmins discovered policy breaches through incidents, not monitoring.

The question we had to answerHow might we let IT enforce security across three operating systems without employees refusing to enrol, and without a single click locking the company out?

03Research & discovery

I went in to validate a complexity hypothesis. Research contradicted it.

Recruitment

Who

IT managers and security analysts at organisations of 200–2,000 employees, each managing at least two OS types. BYOD participants recruited separately.

Protocol

How

Contextual inquiry, observing admins live in their existing tools, then semi-structured follow-ups on policy configuration and incident response.

Synthesis

Making sense

Affinity mapping across about 180 observations, then policy-tree mapping to reconcile OS coverage into one schema.

Evaluation

Testing

Moderated usability testing with 5 IT admins on the shipped flows. BYOD enrolment tested separately, with willingness captured before and after.

Key findings

  1. Employees weren’t confused. They didn’t believe it.

    They completed the enrolment flow and then declined. MDM tools are opaque about what they monitor, so people assume the worst.

    Enrolment testing

  2. Violations surfaced through incidents, not monitoring.

    Proactive alerting was the most requested capability across every admin interview.

    Admin interviews

  3. Seventy-three equal options hide the four that matter.

    Shown a flat list, admins couldn’t tell the settings that mattered from the ones that didn’t.

    Usability testing

  4. Disappearing options erode trust in the tool itself.

    When settings vanished, admins stopped trusting that a policy had actually been applied.

    Usability testing

  5. Nothing showed the blast radius of a click.

    One admin described a policy that hit the wrong device group and locked several hundred people out of their laptops on a Monday morning.

    Contextual inquiry

Limitations, stated up front. Five admins is enough to surface direction, not to size an effect. The 42% → 79% figure measures stated willingness, not real enrolment, and stated intent overstates adoption.

04Product strategy

The brief was about complexity. The product became about trust, twice over.

Employees had to trust IT enough to hand over a device. Admins had to trust the tool enough to press a button that could affect hundreds of laptops. Every major decision follows from that reframe.

Bet 01

Earn belief before asking for the device

Show exactly what IT can and can never see, at the moment of decision, instead of making the flow faster.

Bet 02

One intent, three translations

Admins describe a policy once. The system translates it into each operating system’s enforcement.

Bet 03

Friction proportional to consequence

Low-risk actions stay one click. Fleet-wide, irreversible actions slow down and show who they affect.

Bet 04

Triage first, browse second

Open on what needs doing now, not the full state of the fleet.

Scope as a product decision

We chose73 policies mapped to real compliance audits
OverMatching competitors’ 200+ policies
BecauseAdmins used about a dozen of those. Every extra option is one more thing to misconfigure.
We choseOne unified policy model
OverThree per-OS configuration screens
BecauseSeparate screens are how policy drifts between platforms in the first place.
We choseAdding a fifth enrolment step

BecauseSpeed wasn’t the blocker. Belief was.

As acting PM I decided which of several hundred possible device controls made the first release, in what order, and which OS reached parity when.

05Information architecture

Four jobs, one console, and a translation layer underneath.

System architecture

Admin interfaceUnified dashboard · Policy builder · Device inventory · Compliance monitor · Remote actions
Policy engineValidator · Conflict resolver · Rollout scheduler · Compliance engine · Alerting
OS connectorsAndroid EMM API · Windows MDM / Intune · Apple MDM protocol
Device fleetCorporate-owned · BYOD enrolled · Kiosk mode · Zero-touch provisioned

Why a designer drew this. The policy engine is where conflict resolution, rollout staging and the shape of an alert get decided. They’re interface problems expressed as architecture. If I’d waited for engineering to define that layer, I’d have been designing screens on top of decisions I didn’t make.

06UX & UI design

Four decisions that defined the product.

Decision 01 · BYOD enrolmentTested · n=5

I added a step to raise adoption.

The usual advice is to remove steps. I added one: a screen that lists in plain language what IT can see and, more importantly, what it can never see.

v1 · optimise speed

Four steps, under two minutes. 42% stated willingness to enrol.

What testing showed

No one failed a task. They finished the flow, then said they wouldn’t do it with their real phone.

Shipped · optimise belief

A fifth step with two columns, shown at the moment of decision. 79% stated willingness.

Principle

Friction isn’t the problem. Friction that doesn’t explain itself is. A step that answers the question stopping the user is the most important part of the flow.

Decision 02 · Destructive actionsDesign scope

Show the blast radius, not “Are you sure?”

The tool did exactly what it was told when it locked several hundred people out. Nothing on screen had shown how many devices the click would hit.

Industry default

“This cannot be undone.” People click through it on reflex. It protects the vendor, not the admin.

Why it fails

It asks for confirmation without giving information. The admin needs to see that their filter caught 340 devices, not 4.

Shipped · proportional

Named devices and owners, typed confirmation that scales with severity, and an undo window before the command goes out.

Try Remote wipe on All Windows · 340. The undo window is real.

Principle

Friction should match the consequence, and it should tell you something. Showing 340 names to someone who expected 4 is the safety mechanism.

Decision 03 · Policy architectureTested

One policy form for three operating systems.

Some policies apply everywhere, like password length. Others exist on one OS only: BitLocker on Windows, AirDrop on Apple, factory-reset protection on Android. I explored three structures.

Principle

Complexity should be available, not constantly visible. Fading instead of hiding kept admins’ trust that a setting existed and had applied.

Decision 04 · Compliance monitorTested

Triage first, browse second.

Admins arrive asking “what do I need to do right now?”, not “what is the state of my fleet?” Those questions need almost opposite interfaces.

Principle

“Give users all the data and let them decide” sounds respectful but often means giving up. Deciding what deserves attention is the design work.

07Key features

What shipped, and why each part exists.

◎

Transparent BYOD enrolment

Work profiles with explicit, visible boundaries between work and personal data, shown before enrolment completes.

⚙

Unified policy builder

73 policies across three OSes in one form, grouped by intent, with platform coverage visible at a glance.

⚠

Remote actions with blast radius

Lock, wipe, locate and reset, with named targets, severity-scaled confirmation and an undo window.

▤

Compliance monitor

A severity-ranked queue with remediation next to each issue, plus fleet-wide state per OS.

⇄

Policy conflict resolution

Detects contradictory policies across overlapping device groups before rollout.

◷

Rollout scheduling

Staged deployment with per-wave monitoring instead of fleet-wide pushes.

✓

Zero-touch enrolment

Devices provisioned and configured without IT touching them; under 5 minutes in testing.

▦

Kiosk mode

Single-purpose lockdown for shop floor and field devices.

☰

Audit logs & reporting

Every action timestamped and attributed; posture reports mapped to the frameworks customers are audited against.

08Challenges & solutions

The hard parts, and what we did about them.

Challenge

Two users with opposing interests

Admins want control; employees want privacy. Serving only the buyer stalls adoption.

How we solved it

Make the boundary explicit

A plain-language “can see / can never see” step, and work profiles that keep personal data out of IT’s reach.

Challenge

One intent, three enforcement models

Each OS exposes different controls through different APIs, so policy drifted between platforms.

How we solved it

A single schema with a translation layer

Admins configure intent once; the policy engine maps it to Android, Windows and Apple connectors.

Challenge

Irreversible actions at fleet scale

A mis-scoped filter can lock hundreds of people out, and generic confirmations get clicked through.

How we solved it

Informative, proportional friction

Blast-radius preview with names, typed confirmation for severe actions, and a real undo window.

Challenge

Too much accurate data

The complete fleet view was correct and still ignored.

How we solved it

Prioritise for the admin

A severity-ranked queue as the default view, with remediation inline.

Challenge

A small research sample

Five admins can show direction, not effect size.

How we solved it

Disclose it, every time

Sample sizes and limitations appear next to the numbers, here and in interviews.

What this taught me

High-stakes interfaces need deliberate friction

Low friction is a value, not a law. Irreversibility is when you trade it away.

B2B products have two users

The buyer and the user can want opposite things. Design for both, or adoption stalls.

Task success can hide the real failure

Every participant completed v1. I now ask “would you?” as well as “can you?”, because only one predicts adoption.

What I’d do differently

Back the headline number with behaviour. A larger unmoderated study or production enrolment data would turn a strong signal into a claim without a caveat.
