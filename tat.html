TAT Genie: a queue that knows what matters.

Problem

The queue was sorted by arrival time. SLA breaches were found in Monday’s report, a coordinator routed every ticket by hand, and an AI route could start the clock in the wrong place.

What I did

Re-sorted the queue by time to breach, gated AI routing on confidence, turned agent corrections into training data, explained every auto-assignment, and let people describe problems instead of classifying them.

What changed

25,000+ tickets on the live platform. Four tools became one record model, and breach risk now shows up before a breach happens.

01Project overview

One service desk for everything employees need from IT, and a queue that decides what happens next.

TAT Genie unifies the four ITIL disciplines (incident, problem, change and service request) on one record model, across web and mobile, with AI that classifies and routes incoming tickets.

Primary user

Service desk agents

Work the queue all day. The good ones prioritised from memory and instinct.

Primary user

Desk managers

Accountable for SLAs, and found out about breaches after the fact.

Secondary user

Employees

File tickets, and don’t know or care what ITIL calls their problem.

Secondary user

Fulfilment engineers

Receive assignments, and want to know why a ticket landed with them.

Business context

The brief was consolidation: four disconnected tools, manual routing and no unified reporting. Those were real problems and the ones the buyer cared about. The product also had its own PM, so my job included influencing a roadmap I didn’t own.

02Problem

The queue was sorted by when things arrived, not by what they would cost.

“I find out we’ve breached an SLA when the report runs on Monday. By then it’s already someone else’s escalation.”

Service desk manager · discovery interview

4 toolsacross the ticket lifecycleIncident, change, requests and knowledge in separate systems, with no shared record.
Manualrouting on arrivalA coordinator read each ticket and assigned it by hand: a queue in front of the queue.
After the factSLA visibilityBreaches surfaced in weekly reporting, after the cost had already been paid.

The question we had to answerHow might we make the next right action obvious to every agent, new or senior, and let AI take work off their plate without hiding its mistakes?

03Research & discovery

Sitting with agents surfaced something the brief never mentioned.

Method

Observation with agents

Sitting with agents as they worked the live queue, watching how they actually chose the next ticket.

Method

Discovery interviews

Desk managers on SLAs, escalation and reporting.

Method

Testing shipped flows

Watching agents use the first version of the queue, and engineers react to assignment.

Key findings

  1. The order agents needed wasn’t in the system.

    Every list and filter was sorted by arrival. Agents re-prioritised in their heads on every refresh, and quality collapsed when a senior agent took leave.

    Observation · three sessions

  2. Breach risk was invisible until it was too late.

    Managers learned about breaches from retrospective reports.

    Manager interview

  3. Round-robin is fair by count, not by load.

    It kept sending hardware requests to engineers with no asset-store access, and ignored that two open P1s aren’t equal to five password resets.

    Shipped v1

  4. Nobody filing a ticket knows ITIL.

    A type picker on step one meant employees guessed, guessed wrong, and the reporting built on that field became worthless.

  5. People search the knowledge base once, then file.

    And the first agent reply was often a link the person had already read.

  6. Rules nobody can read never get switched on.

    Every desk tool ships a rules engine; almost nobody used the advanced half, because a silent failure affects everyone.

04Product strategy

Time is the material this product is made of.

Bet 01

Sort by consequence, not arrival

The default order is a product decision. Ordering by time-to-breach puts the next right action in row one.

Bet 02

Automation is a threshold, not a switch

The useful question was never “should AI route?” but “above what confidence?”

Bet 03

Make the reasoning visible

Auto-assignment and AI routing show why, so people can argue with them instead of routing around them.

Bet 04

Describe first, classify later

The ITIL taxonomy serves reporting, so it lives behind the agent view, not on the employee’s first screen.

Influencing without owning the backlog

The queue finding started as an observation from three research sessions and ended up changing the roadmap, because I could show it working rather than assert it. I also argued for folding the self-service portal and knowledge base into ticket creation instead of shipping them as separate modules. That’s what shipped.

We choseConfidence-gated AI routing
OverFull automation, or approving every ticket
BecauseFull automation hides mistakes until they cost SLA time; approving everything reinstates the coordinator bottleneck.
We choseSelf-service inside ticket creation
OverA separate self-service portal
BecausePeople don’t browse a knowledge base. They search once, fail, and file.
We chosePlain-language WHEN / IF / THEN rules
OverA full boolean condition tree
BecauseThe rules people need are simple. The ones they can’t read are the ones that cause incidents.

06UX & UI design

Five decisions that carried the product.

Decision 01 · The queueObserved in testing

Sort by consequence, not arrival.

A P4 laptop request open for nine hours is less urgent than a P1 outage opened twelve minutes ago with eight minutes of SLA left. Sorting by age buries the ticket that matters.

v1 · by age

The convention, and I shipped it without thinking. Agents scrolled the whole list, then asked a colleague what to pick.

What the scroll meant

They weren’t browsing. They were prioritising in their heads on every refresh.

v2 · by time-to-breach

Live countdowns and severity colour. The first row is always the next thing to work on.

Principle

However you order a list tells people what to care about, and most lists are ordered by whatever was easiest to index.

Decision 02 · AI triageAdoption observed

The AI proposes. The agent decides. Each disagreement becomes training data.

A routing decision starts a clock. A wrong route burns SLA time in the wrong queue, so the ticket reaches the right team already late.

Rejected · full automation

Fastest, and the model’s mistakes stay invisible until they’ve cost time.

Rejected · approve everything

Safe, and it brings back the bottleneck we were removing.

Shipped · confidence-gated

Above the threshold, tickets route automatically. Below it, a human decides, seeing which signals matched.

Principle

Saving every override as a labelled example changed how agents saw it: correcting the model felt like teaching it, not cleaning up after it.

Decision 03 · Service requestsDesign scope

Explain every auto-assignment.

The shipped model filters by skill, then shift, then weighted load, where a P1 counts far more than a routine request, and it shows every step. Auto-assignment fails politically before it fails technically: the first question an engineer asks is “why did I get this?”

Decision 04 · Workflow automationDesign scope

Automation people can read is automation people will switch on.

The builder reads as a sentence (when this happens, if these conditions hold, then do this) and every rule shows what it did the last time it ran.

Decision 05 · Intake and self-serviceDesign scope

Describe first, classify later, and deflect inline.

Everyone starts with a plain description. The system proposes a type and agents can reclassify. Matching articles appear while the person types; if they still file, the articles they rejected are attached so the agent doesn’t send them again. Tickets filed after a failed search flag that article as insufficient.

Principle

Never ask users to classify something using your internal model of the domain.

07Key features

Fourteen modules; these are the ones that changed how the desk works.

⏱

Consequence-sorted queue

Tickets ordered by SLA remaining, with live countdowns and severity colour.

◎

Confidence-gated AI triage

Classification, priority and routing with confidence shown; overrides saved as training examples.

⇄

Explainable assignment

Skill → shift → weighted load, every step visible to the engineer.

⚙

Readable workflow automation

WHEN / IF / THEN rules, each with a visible run history.

✎

Describe-first intake

Plain-language tickets from portal, email, chat or Teams and Slack; type proposed by the system.

☰

In-flow knowledge

Articles surface while typing; rejected ones travel with the ticket.

◷

SLA engine

Per-priority targets, business-hours calendars, pause states and escalation ladders.

⚑

Problem & change management

Root-cause records linked to recurring incidents; planned changes with approvals and rollback plans.

▤

Analytics & mobile

Volume, resolution, SLA attainment and load dashboards; on-call triage and approvals on mobile.

08Challenges & solutions

The hard parts, and what we did about them.

Challenge

AI routing starts a clock

A wrong route costs SLA time before anyone notices.

How we solved it

A confidence threshold

Route automatically only above 80%; below it, a human decides with the model’s reasoning visible.

Challenge

Agents distrust automation they can’t question

“Why did I get this?” has to have an answer.

How we solved it

Show the reasoning

Assignment and triage display the signals and filters behind every decision.

Challenge

The ITIL taxonomy versus real people

Employees can’t classify; misfiled tickets corrupt reporting.

How we solved it

Move the taxonomy behind the agent view

Describe first; propose a type; experts reclassify with history kept.

Challenge

Changing a roadmap I didn’t own

A research observation isn’t a priority until a PM believes it.

How we solved it

Show, don’t assert

A working demonstration of the re-sorted queue made the case. The demo on this page is the same argument.

Challenge

Proving impact

Resolution-time and SLA-attainment gains weren’t instrumented before and after.

How we solved it

What this taught me

Default sort order is a product decision

Conventions are decisions somebody else made in a different context, that you inherit for free and pay for later.

Automation needs a threshold, not an on switch

Framing it as a threshold turned an argument about beliefs into a tuning conversation with real data.

Make correcting the system feel like teaching it

Users tolerate an imperfect system far better when their corrections visibly go somewhere.

What I’d do differently

Instrument on day one. The triage redesign is the change I’m most confident worked, and the one I can least prove. Next time the success metric goes in the spec before the first frame.
