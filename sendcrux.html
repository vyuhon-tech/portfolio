Sendcrux & WarmupIP: automate the labour, not the understanding.

Whether an email reaches the inbox depends on systems the sender can’t see: DNS authentication, sender reputation, spam filters. I designed the warm-up engine that builds reputation, then the platform built around it, for salespeople rather than engineers.

Problem

Users didn’t understand SPF, DKIM or DMARC, and they were afraid of permanently damaging their domain. Setup took hours, and warm-up was a black box they had to trust for weeks.

What I did

Automated every setup step but showed each one as a verified line item, paired the warm-up schedule with the reputation it protects, and designed one information architecture for the whole stack.

What changed

Time to first campaign fell from 2–3 hours to about 15 minutes, and domain setup from about 2 hours to about 2 minutes. Both are measured, and both products are live.

01Project overview

Two products, one system: the reputation engine, then the platform around it.

WarmupIP builds sender reputation safely. It ramps sending volume gradually over several weeks while a seed network generates authentic engagement, so a new domain can send at volume without being flagged.

Sendcrux came next. Users were stitching five to seven tools together around warm-up (domain registrars, mailbox providers, DNS, warm-up and outreach), so Sendcrux absorbed that stack into one platform.

Primary user

Sales teams doing outbound

“I just want to send emails. I don’t want to become a DNS expert.”

Primary user

Growth marketers

Have plenty of data and not enough insight about what to do next.

Channel

Agencies & partners

Resell the infrastructure through white label and a partner programme.

The products today

Both are live. Sendcrux’s public site describes an outreach platform that cycles across ESPs, SMTPs and domains, with domain and DNS automation, mailbox licence management, multi-step sequences and bounce handling across providers. WarmupIP now positions itself around enterprise deliverability.

Public positioning from sendcrux.com and warmupip.com, checked November 2026. Both products have evolved since my tenure ended in November 2024; this case study covers only my work.

04Product strategy

Automate the work. Expose the reasoning.

The obvious answer to confused users is to hide complexity. But these users were also frightened, and hiding things from frightened people makes them more anxious, not less. The strategy resolved that tension rather than picking a side.

Bet 01

Automate the labour, never the understanding

Generate and verify every record automatically, and show each one as a named, verified line item.

Bet 02

Pair activity with outcome

Never show the warm-up ramp without the reputation it’s building.

Bet 03

Narrate the uncertain parts

When a chart looks flat on purpose, say so before the user worries.

Bet 04

Design the primitive, then the system

Warm-up taught us the domain. The platform around it revealed where the domain’s real seams were.

We choseA verified checklist of every DNS record
OverOne “Set up my domain” button with a spinner
BecauseThe same time, but only the checklist leaves users able to answer “is my infrastructure correct?” without support.
We choseSchedule plus inbox placement
Over“Warm-up 40% complete”
BecauseProgress bars measure the system’s activity. Users needed to know whether it was working.
We choseOne information architecture for twelve surfaces
OverDesigning each feature on its own
BecauseA salesperson can move from warming a domain to building a sequence without relearning anything.
05Information architecture

Twelve surfaces, one mental model.

Three layers

InterfaceDashboard, campaign builder, analytics and settings: what the user touches.
PlatformInfrastructure engine (domains, mailboxes, warm-up), outreach engine (sequences, scheduling, leads) and analytics engine.
ExternalEmail providers, SMTP, spam filters and DNS: systems we depend on and can’t control, which is exactly why their state has to be surfaced.
06UX & UI design

Four decisions that made invisible infrastructure legible.

Decision 01 · Domain setupMeasured before / after

A verified checklist instead of a spinner.

I nearly shipped one “Set up my domain” button with a spinner. It was the fastest possible flow, and it gave an anxious user nothing to check at the moment they were most afraid of breaking something permanent.

The trap

Full automation behind a spinner. Fast, and impossible to verify.

The old way

Generate records by hand, paste them into a registrar, wait and guess. About two hours.

Shipped

Every record generated and verified automatically, each shown as a named line item with live status.

Principle

Automate the labour, never the understanding. A spinner and a verified checklist take the same time; only one leaves the user able to trust the result.

Decision 02 · Warm-up engineTeam-observed

Show the ramp, show what it’s protecting, and explain the flat part.

Users had to leave warm-up running for a month while trusting it wasn’t damaging the thing they were most afraid of damaging. The first five days look like nothing is happening, and that’s exactly when anxious users churn.

Principle

Labelling the flat start as expected, instead of letting a flat line speak for itself, was the most valuable copy change in the product.

Decision 03 · ScopeDesign scope

Designing the primitive, then the system around it.

WarmupIP was about making one invisible process legible. Sendcrux was about sequencing five processes so a user never has to understand where one ends and the next begins, while still being able to diagnose which one broke. Twelve surfaces, one design language and one mental model, designed once for the whole stack rather than per feature.

Decision 04 · Campaign executionDesign scope

The sending that didn’t happen.

A reply immediately removes that lead from every remaining step. It sounds obvious, but nothing damages a pipeline faster than a prospect who replied getting two more automated follow-ups.

08Challenges & solutions

The hard parts, and what we did about them.

Challenge

Complex infrastructure, anxious non-experts

Simplifying would calm confused users and alarm frightened ones.

How we solved it

Legible automation

Automate every step, and show each as a verified, named item users can check themselves.

Challenge

A month-long process that looks idle at first

Flat early reputation looks like failure.

How we solved it

Narrate expected behaviour

Phase labels on the ramp, and reputation always shown next to the schedule.

Challenge

Five to seven tools in a chain

Each tool had its own model, and failures were hard to locate.

How we solved it

One system, one IA

Sendcrux sequenced the stack while keeping each layer’s state visible for diagnosis.

Challenge

Users outgrow onboarding fast

Designs tuned for first-run had to be retrofitted for experts.

How we solved it

Design the expert path from the start

Per-mailbox schedules, custom ramps and granular controls.

Challenge

Attributing outcomes honestly

Reply rates and revenue depend mostly on copy and list quality, not the interface.

How we solved it

Claim only what design moved

Setup-time numbers are reported; revenue and reply-rate figures are deliberately left out.

What this taught me

Anxious users need more information, not less

Simplifying and reassuring are different design goals. Fear needs detail you can verify.

Design the expert path from the start

Users don’t stay novices. Designing only the beginner path guarantees a rebuild.

Analytics were the retention feature

Seeing reputation improve was what made the product feel like it was working. The dashboard wasn’t reporting on the product; it was the product.

What I’d do differently

I originally split this into two portfolio pieces, and that’s a useful tell. When two case studies share a problem and a metric set, you’ve mistaken two products for two design problems. It was always one.
