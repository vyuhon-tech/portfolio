QA Genie: when the AI generates too much.

A platform where AI writes test cases and humans decide which ones are real. Generating them was the easy half. Reviewing forty AI-written cases one at a time is slower than writing ten yourself, and that became the product’s real problem.

Problem

“It gave me forty test cases in nine seconds. It took me two hours to work out which twelve I actually trusted, and my name is on the ones I approve.”

What I did

Changed the success measure from time-to-generate to time-to-a-reviewed-suite. Grouped cases for batch approval, blocked uncertain cases from any batch, and put QA and automation on one lifecycle record.

What changed

Shipped scope: 11 modules, five reviewable case types, and traceability from a case’s origin to its execution evidence. No outcome metrics are claimed yet.

01Project overview

AI writes the test cases. Humans stay accountable for which ones are real.

QA Genie generates test cases for a target application, takes them through human review, hands approved cases to automation developers who write Playwright (Python) scripts, and executes them with captured evidence.

It generates five kinds of case (functional, positive, negative, boundary and validation) from three starting points: an existing Excel suite, AI discovery of a URL, or manual authoring.

Primary user

QA engineers

Professionally accountable for the suite they sign off, so they can’t rubber-stamp forty cases.

Primary user

Automation developers

Turn approved cases into scripts, unit-test them and submit them back to QA.

Stakeholder

Anyone asking about coverage

Needs an answer to “what’s actually covered?” without assembling it by hand.

Business context

QA teams were working across Excel, Jira and manual reporting. Test cases lived in spreadsheets and automation status lived in someone’s head. AI generation was the obvious answer and it worked immediately, which is exactly what created the new problem.

02Problem

Generation was never the bottleneck. Accountability was.

The question we had to answerHow might we make a human’s review of machine output fast, without making it careless?

Those two goals actively pull against each other. Most of the work on this product was about resolving that tension.

03Research & discovery

One prototype session changed what we measured.

Method

Prototype sessions with QA engineers

I ran the sessions, putting generated suites in front of the people who would have to sign them off.

Method

Testing the first build

Watching how reviewers actually handled a flat list of forty generated cases.

Limitation

No post-launch data yet

This is the newest product. Findings below come from sessions and testing, not production behaviour.

Key findings

  1. Time-to-generate was already near zero.

    The number that mattered was time to a reviewed suite, and our first build made that worse than the spreadsheet it replaced.

    First prototype session

  2. The reviewer’s name is on every approval.

    A QA engineer is accountable for the suite they sign off, so speed can’t come at the cost of scrutiny.

    Prototype sessions

  3. A flat list forces a bad choice.

    Reviewers either read everything and lost two hours, or skimmed and approved in bulk, which is worse because the sign-off stops meaning anything.

    Testing the first build

  4. Most cases are boilerplate. A few are genuinely uncertain.

    The model gets most cases right every time. A small number are near-duplicates of existing coverage, or need domain judgement it doesn’t have.

    Prototype sessions

  5. Two teams had no shared definition of “handed off”.

    QA and automation each had a coherent view of their own work, and the handoff lived in chat.

  6. Nobody adopts a QA platform that starts them at zero.

    Teams arrive with years of cases in spreadsheets. Importing that history is how adoption starts.

04Product strategy

Measure the reviewed suite, then route human attention to where the machine is weakest.

Reframe

Time-to-a-reviewed-suite, not time-to-generate

Optimising generation speed was optimising a number that was already near zero.

Bet 01

Batch the obvious, isolate the uncertain

Let people approve boilerplate in one action, and make it impossible to sweep uncertain cases in with it.

Bet 02

One record, two faces

QA and automation see the same lifecycle, with ownership visibly passing between them.

Bet 03

Converge every intake immediately

Three ways in, one place to land, one thing to learn.

Defining where AI acts and where a human must

As acting PM I defined the workflow model: where AI acts, where a human must, and what a completed handoff between QA and automation actually means.

We choseGrouped review with flagged cases excluded from batches
OverA flat list with select-all
BecauseSelect-all turns a slow process into a careless one. The speed and the accountability loss come from the same control.
We choseOne lifecycle record with shifting ownership
OverSeparate QA and developer workspaces
BecauseThe moment you fork the data model, the two teams start disagreeing about what “done” means.
We choseExcel import as a first-class path
OverStarting every team from zero
BecauseMigration is the adoption path. The import is a trust feature that looks like a utility.

06UX & UI design

Three decisions that made the AI usable.

Decision 01 · Review workspacePrototype-tested

Spend human attention where the machine is weakest.

My first build treated every AI output as equally deserving of scrutiny: forty rows, forty identical approve and reject controls. They aren’t equal.

Approve a whole group in one click. The flagged cases won’t be included.

Principle

Batch actions and careful review aren’t opposites. It’s a routing problem. The real design work is deciding what counts as uncertain.

Decision 02 · The handoffDesign scope

One record, two roles.

An approved case isn’t finished. It becomes a work item for an automation developer. In the old process, that handoff happened over chat and left no trace.

Principle

Two-sided products fail at the seam. Design the seam, not just each side.

Decision 03 · IntakeDesign scope

Three ways in, one thing to learn.

Teams arrive with an existing Excel sheet, a URL they want explored, or a case they’ll write by hand. The obvious build is three flows, which is also three products to learn. All three routes deposit cases into the same review workspace in the same state, so what happens next is identical however a case arrived.

Principle

When a product has multiple entry points, converge them as early as possible. Divergent intake is fine; divergent workflow multiplies everything downstream.

07Key features

Eleven modules; these are the ones that make AI output trustworthy.

▦

Grouped review & approval

Cases grouped by type; batch approval for the obvious; flagged cases always reviewed individually; edit in place.

⇄

Shared lifecycle record

Seven defined states with ownership moving between QA and automation, and rejection reasons kept on the record.

◎

Traceability

Every case linked from its origin through approval, script, run and outcome.

✦

AI test generation

Functional, positive, negative, boundary and validation cases from a target.

⌕

URL discovery

Crawls a target route and proposes coverage from what it finds.

⇪

Excel import

Brings existing suites in with column mapping and validation.

⚙

Automation pipeline & scripts

Approved cases become work items; Playwright (Python) scripts versioned against their source case.

▶

Execution & evidence

Run single scripts or full suites, scheduled or on demand, with screenshots and logs captured per run.

▤

Analytics dashboard

Pass/fail trends, automation coverage and productivity tracking.

08Challenges & solutions

The hard parts, and what we did about them.

Challenge

Speed and diligence come from the same control

Batch approval makes review fast and careless at once.

How we solved it

Decide what can’t be batched

Flagged cases (near-duplicates, low confidence, domain judgement) are excluded from batches by design.

Challenge

AI output volume as a design constraint

Earlier AI features produced one output at a time. This one produced forty artifacts a person is accountable for.

How we solved it

Design the review, not just the generation

The interface for reviewing output mattered more than the model’s quality.

Challenge

Two roles, two vocabularies

QA and automation each had a coherent view of their own work and no shared definition of done.

How we solved it

One record with two faces

Defined states, visible ownership, and rejection history on the record.

Challenge

Adoption from zero coverage

Teams won’t switch to a tool that ignores years of existing cases.

How we solved it

Make migration first-class

Excel import with mapping and validation, and provenance kept per case.

Challenge

No production data yet

It’s tempting to estimate time savings to make the outcomes look complete.

How we solved it

Report scope, not guesses

Outcomes below are countable and verifiable, and labelled as scope.

What this taught me

Generation volume is a design constraint, not a feature

When AI produces artifacts a person is accountable for, the review interface matters more than the model.

Efficiency and diligence come from the same control

Deciding what gets excluded from a batch is the design work. The checkbox is trivial.

Two-sided products fail at the seam

One record with two faces was harder than two workspaces, and it’s why the handoff stopped happening in chat.

What I’d do differently

Run the first prototype session before building the flat list. The insight that time-to-generate was already near zero was available from one conversation, and I spent a build cycle learning it.
